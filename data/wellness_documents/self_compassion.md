---
title: Self-Compassion
topic: shame
---
Self-compassion means treating yourself with the same kindness you would offer a friend who is struggling. Research by Kristin Neff describes three parts: self-kindness instead of self-judgment, common humanity instead of isolation, and mindfulness instead of over-identification with painful feelings.

The self-compassion break. When you notice distress, say to yourself: 1) "This is a moment of suffering" or "This is really hard right now." 2) "Struggle is part of being human; other people feel this too." 3) "May I be kind to myself in this moment." Placing a hand on your chest can help.

Mistakes and guilt. Guilt says "I did something bad" and can motivate repair. Shame says "I am bad" and tends to make us hide. Try separating the behavior from your identity: "I missed the deadline. That doesn't make me a failure. What can I do now to fix it?"

Self-compassion is not self-pity or making excuses. People who are kinder to themselves after setbacks are often more likely to take responsibility and try again, because failure feels less threatening.

Feeling not good enough. Notice the inner critic's voice and give it a name. Ask whether its standards are ones you would apply to anyone else. Write a short letter to yourself from the perspective of a caring friend.
