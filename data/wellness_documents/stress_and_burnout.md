---
title: Stress and Burnout
topic: stress
---
Stress is a normal response to demands. Short-term stress can sharpen focus, but long-term stress without recovery can lead to burnout: exhaustion, cynicism or detachment, and a sense that nothing you do is effective.

Signs of burnout. Feeling drained most days, dreading tasks you once enjoyed, irritability, trouble concentrating, getting sick more often, and changes in sleep or appetite.

Prioritize with the Eisenhower matrix. Sort tasks into urgent and important (do now), important but not urgent (schedule), urgent but not important (delegate or minimize), and neither (drop). When overwhelmed, choose just the next one task.

Set boundaries. It is okay to say no or "not right now." Try: "I'd like to help, but I don't have capacity this week." Protect time for sleep, meals and rest as non-negotiable.

Build in recovery. Short breaks every 60 to 90 minutes, movement, time outdoors, and activities unrelated to work or school help the nervous system recover. Rest is part of productivity, not the opposite of it.

Talk to someone. Sharing what you're dealing with, with a friend, mentor, advisor or counselor, can reduce the load. Universities often offer academic accommodations and extensions for students experiencing serious stress.
