---
title: Cognitive Reframing (CBT Skills)
topic: thoughts
---
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is based on the idea that thoughts, feelings and behaviors influence one another. Changing how we interpret a situation can change how we feel about it.

Common thinking traps. All-or-nothing thinking ("If I don't get an A, I'm a failure"). Catastrophizing (expecting the worst). Mind reading (assuming others think badly of you). Overgeneralizing ("This always happens to me"). Should statements ("I should be able to handle this"). Emotional reasoning ("I feel stupid, so I must be stupid").

The thought record. 1) Describe the situation briefly. 2) Write the automatic thought. 3) Name the emotion and rate it 0-100. 4) List evidence for the thought. 5) List evidence against it. 6) Write a more balanced thought. 7) Re-rate the emotion.

Balanced thoughts are not forced positivity. Instead of "Everything will be fine," try "This is hard, and I have handled hard things before. I can ask for help with the parts I can't do alone."

The friend test. Ask yourself what you would say to a good friend who had this thought. We are often far kinder and more realistic with others than with ourselves.

Behavioral activation. When mood is low, we tend to withdraw, which lowers mood further. Schedule small activities that bring a sense of pleasure or accomplishment, such as a short walk, a call with a friend, or finishing one small task, even if motivation is low. Action often comes before motivation.
