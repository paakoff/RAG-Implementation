---
title: Managing Anxiety and Worry
topic: anxiety
---
Anxiety is the body's alarm system. It prepares us to respond to threat with a faster heartbeat, quicker breathing, tense muscles and racing thoughts. It becomes a problem when the alarm goes off often, loudly, or when there is no real danger.

Scheduled worry time. Pick a 15-minute "worry window" each day. When a worry shows up outside that window, write it down and tell yourself you will give it attention later. During the window, review the list and ask which worries are solvable. Many people find worries feel smaller by the time the window arrives.

Separate solvable from unsolvable worries. For a solvable worry, write one next action you can take, even a tiny one, such as emailing a professor or making a study plan. For a worry you cannot solve right now, practice noticing it ("I'm having the thought that...") and gently returning attention to the present.

Check the evidence. Anxious thoughts often overestimate how likely something bad is and underestimate how well we would cope. Ask: What is the evidence for and against this thought? What is the most realistic outcome? If the worst did happen, what would I do and who could help?

Test anxiety and exam stress. Break study into 25-minute focused blocks with 5-minute breaks. Practice under test-like conditions to reduce uncertainty. On the day, arrive early, use slow breathing, and if your mind goes blank, skip to a question you know and come back.

Seek extra support if anxiety lasts most days for several weeks, causes panic attacks, or makes you avoid classes, work or relationships. Campus counseling centers and primary care providers can help.
